    Evidence-informed guidance

    Choose the right independence route

    Different dogs cope differently with confinement, rooms, home exits and outdoor spaces.

    Open PDF guide
    1

    Pen or enclosed area

    Use this route when the dog already views the pen, playpen or gated safe area positively. The route starts with calmness nearby and gradually adds distance and absences.

    2

    Another room and home exits

    Use a dog-safe room, baby gate or closed door before progressing to keys, shoes, front-door exits and true home-alone practice.

    3

    Secure outdoor practice

    Outdoor practice is optional and must use a private, escape-proof, weather-safe area with water, shade or shelter and close monitoring.

    !

    Confinement distress is different

    A dog may panic in a pen but remain calm loose in a safe room. Video different setups and choose the route in which the dog is physically safe and emotionally calmer.

    Before every session

    • Offer a toilet opportunity and meet age-appropriate exercise and rest needs.
    • Choose a safe location with comfortable temperature, water and no accessible hazards.
    • Start below the point at which the dog usually becomes distressed.
    • Use a camera or audio monitor for true departures whenever possible.
    • Keep departures and returns calm and ordinary.

    How to respond to distress

    What you observeAdjustment
    Brief whine or one or two barks, followed by recoveryReward the calm pause and make the next repetition easy.
    Repeated vocalising but still eating and pausingReduce duration or distance by about half.
    Continuous sharper crying, frantic scratching, escape attempts or refusing foodReturn calmly, end the challenge and move to a much easier plan.
    Sudden difficulty after previous successCheck toileting, fatigue, pain, temperature, noise and other practical needs.

    Outdoor safety is non-negotiable

    • Use only a secure private area with reliable fencing and locked gates.
    • Do not train outdoors in dangerous heat, cold, storms or direct sun without shade.
    • Provide clean water and suitable shelter.
    • Do not leave a dog outdoors if there is an escape, theft, poisoning or neighbour-conflict risk.
    • Outdoor practice is not a substitute for teaching calm home-alone time.

    When professional help is appropriate

    Seek a veterinarian or qualified clinical animal behaviour professional when the dog panics rapidly, injures themselves, destroys exits, drools heavily, toilets from distress, cannot eat when alone, or cannot progress despite careful sub-threshold training.
